Most football arguments start the same way. Someone says a player was the best this season. 
Someone else says another player deserved it more. Then come the trophies, the headlines, the reputation, the goals, the big nights, the memories. 
Everyone has an answer. Almost no one has the same reason. That is part of what makes football beautiful. 
But it is also what makes some individual awards so difficult to trust.

Every year, the shortlist is debated. The order is debated. 
The winner is debated. A defender can have an extraordinary season and barely enter the conversation. 
A goalkeeper can decide matches for months and still lose attention to someone who scores more goals. 
A player can be brilliant without playing for the right club, winning the right trophy, or creating the right story at the right moment.

We have become very good at arguing about greatness. We are less good at showing our work. 
Because saying someone was the best is easy. 
But explaining why they were better than everyone else - using undisputed facts - is another story.

Football gives us more evidence than ever before. 
Every match leaves a trail: goals, saves, passes, duels, interceptions, chances created, minutes played, opposition faced, competitions entered, performances repeated week after week. 
None of those numbers can explain football on their own. 
But together, used carefully and honestly, they can tell us much more than a ballot ever could.

The point is not to remove judgment from football. 
That would be impossible. Numbers do not choose what matters. 
People do. The difference is that those choices can be made before the result, written down, and applied the same way to everyone.

Someone still has to decide what matters. How much should a goal count? 
How do you compare an interception with a key pass? 
How much harder was the opposition? How do you judge a goalkeeper and a forward without pretending they are doing the same job?

Those choices matter. So they should not be hidden. 
The formula should be visible. The weights should be visible. 
The data should be visible. If a rule changes, everyone should know. 
If information is missing, it should stay missing instead of quietly becoming a zero. 
If two people disagree with the result, they should at least be able to look at the same evidence and understand how it was reached.

And players should be judged for the jobs they actually do.
A goalkeeper should not need to score goals to be recognised as exceptional. 
A defender should not disappear because defending is harder to put into a highlight reel. 
A midfielder should not be reduced to goals and assists. 
A forward should be rewarded for more than the size of their name.

There does not have to be one best footballer. 
There can be a best goalkeeper. A best defender. A best midfielder. A best forward. 
And instead of waiting until the end of the season for people to remember what happened months ago, the ranking can grow with the season itself. 
Every match adds evidence. Every week changes the picture. 

Someone rises. Someone falls. A great month becomes visible. So does a bad one. 
Reputation cannot carry you forever, because the next match always arrives. 
By the end of the season, there is no secret room. No final ballot. No campaign. 
No PR Team. No bribes to voters. No reveal built on opinion. There is simply the record of what happened. 
The pure performance on the pitch. Every player judge by his own accomplishment.

We know the numbers are not perfect. But they do not have favourites. They do not remember a famous name more fondly. 
They do not vote because of a narrative. They do not care who was expected to win. 
And when we make the human choices behind the numbers explicit, anyone can challenge them, test them and reproduce the result. 
That seems like a better place to start than what we currently have.

So we built **Merit**: If football is going to recognise its best performers, performance should come first.

No votes. Just performance.
